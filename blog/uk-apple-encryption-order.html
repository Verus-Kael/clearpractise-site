The UK Government vs. Your Encryption: What the Apple Order Really Means

20 November 2026 | CPWS

A tribunal has just forced the UK government to admit it secretly ordered Apple to bypass encryption. Your business data was never the government's problem to solve - but it is yours. Here is what happened, and what to actually do about it.

What was confirmed this week

On 19 November, the Investigatory Powers Tribunal ruled that the Home Office must stop denying the existence of a Technical Capability Notice (TCN) issued to Apple. The government had argued that even confirming the order existed would damage national security. The judges disagreed - and their reasoning is worth reading in full, because it says a lot about how these powers get used.

The Home Office's official position was "neither confirm nor deny." But the tribunal found the government had already departed from that policy repeatedly: confirming the notice's existence to journalists, briefing US government officials, and watching it discussed openly by President Trump and his former Director of National Intelligence. You cannot run a secrecy doctrine and leak it at the top, the judges effectively concluded - and the government failed to provide detailed evidence that mere acknowledgement would harm national security at all.

The ruling also cleared the way for Apple's legal challenge - backed by Liberty and Privacy International - to proceed in open court. A later phase will decide how much detail about the order can be published. It said nothing about whether the order itself is lawful; that fight comes next year.

Here is the sequence, because the sequence matters:

  • February 2025: Reports emerge that the Home Office has secretly issued a TCN under the Investigatory Powers Act, demanding access to end-to-end encrypted iCloud data - including, reportedly, American users' backups, not just British ones.
  • February 2025: Rather than weaken encryption for everyone, Apple withdraws Advanced Data Protection - its highest level of iCloud encryption, which even Apple cannot unlock - from UK users entirely, and blocks UK users from re-enabling it. Apple's position: "We have never built a backdoor or master key to any of our products and services and never will."
  • Late 2025: After talks with the US administration, the UK drops the worldwide demand. Reports describe a second, narrower notice understood to restrict its scope to the UK.
  • July 2026: Apple files its current challenge to that narrower notice before the tribunal.
  • 19 November 2026: The tribunal rejects the secrecy claim. The full hearing lands next year.

Why this matters even if you use nothing Apple makes

The technical details are Apple's problem. The precedent is everyone's.

A Technical Capability Notice is not a warrant. A warrant says "hand over this specific person's data, here is the evidence." A TCN says "modify your product so that access is possible on demand - and do not tell anyone." It is an order to change the design of a system that hundreds of millions of people trust, issued in secret, with no public debate about whether the trade-off is worth making.

The tribunal did not rule the order lawful or unlawful. What it ruled is that the public is entitled to know the order exists before anyone gets to decide that question. The argument that just lost - secrecy first, debate never - is the argument your data has been losing quietly for years.

The security industry has said for decades what this case now demonstrates in public: a backdoor does not discriminate. If a mechanism exists to bypass encryption, it exists for whoever finds it - the intended agency, an outside attacker, a rogue insider, a foreign intelligence service. And note what actually moved the needle here: the UK dropped its worldwide demand not because of any technical reassessment, but because the US government objected. The arguments that carried weight were geopolitical, not cryptographic.

The quiet casualty nobody is reporting

Apple removed Advanced Data Protection from UK users in February 2025, and it has not returned. British users' iCloud backups have been running on a lower tier of encryption than everyone else's for nearly two years. Most UK users have no idea. Nothing about this week's ruling changes that.

If your business backs up phones, laptops, or client records to iCloud from UK accounts, your cloud backups are weaker than the same backups would be almost anywhere else on earth - as a side effect of a secret order whose lawfulness has not even been decided yet.

What a business can actually do

  1. Know where your sensitive data lives. If you use iCloud with UK accounts, assume your backups are not end-to-end encrypted. That is not paranoia; it is the documented current state.
  2. Encrypt before the cloud, not after. The lesson of this whole episode: encryption that a service provider can turn off at a government's request is not the strongest encryption available to you. Client-side encryption - locking your data on your own devices before it ever reaches a server - puts the keys in your hands, not Apple's, not Google's, not ours.
  3. Pick jurisdictions deliberately. Where your provider is incorporated decides whose laws apply to your data. UK-based services are subject to TCNs; Swiss-based providers operate under different legal frameworks entirely. That is no longer a fringe concern - it is a procurement question.
  4. Ask your suppliers the Apple question. Any business handling client data should be able to answer: "Could you be compelled to modify your systems to expose our data, and would we ever be told?" If they cannot answer, that silence is the answer.

Where we stand

CPWS exists because of episodes like this one. Nothing online is bulletproof. But every layer where your data can be quietly unlocked by an order you were never meant to see is a layer worth removing.

The full trial lands next year. Whatever it decides about one notice, the design lesson already stands: choose tools and providers whose architecture assumes that someday, someone will knock - and make sure what they find is nothing they can open.
